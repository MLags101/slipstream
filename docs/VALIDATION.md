# Validation against published measurements

WindTunnel is only useful if its numbers mean something. This page compares its drag
predictions with classic wind tunnel measurements, using the same app, defaults and
automatic meshing anyone gets. Nothing was hand-tuned per case.

Every case runs as a **mesh-independence sweep**: coarse, then medium and fine only if
drag still changes by more than 2% on refinement. So each result also shows whether the
answer is limited by mesh resolution.

To reproduce (about 45 minutes of solver time on an 8-core Apple Silicon Mac):

```sh
backend/.venv/bin/python examples/validation/make_models.py
backend/.venv/bin/python examples/validation/run_validation.py   # backend on :8000
backend/.venv/bin/python examples/validation/report.py           # prints the tables below
```

## Summary

| Case | WindTunnel Cd (finest settled mesh) | Measured Cd | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ahmed body, 25° slant | 0.311 | 0.285 | **+9%**, mesh independent |
| Sphere, Re = 1×10⁵ | 0.319 | 0.47 | **−32%**, not yet mesh independent |
| Sphere, Re = 4×10⁶ | 0.261 | ≈ 0.18 | **+45%**, mesh independent |

For shapes whose flow separates at sharp edges (car bodies, frames, fairings with a
cut-off tail), expect drag within about 10%. For smooth rounded shapes, where the
separation point moves with speed, expect errors of 30–45% in either direction; comparing
two designs at the same speed is still meaningful, but the absolute number isn't.

## The cases

| Case | Why it's a good test | Setup in WindTunnel |
|---|---|---|
| Sphere, Re = 1×10⁵ | The most measured shape there is. Laminar boundary layer, early separation, Cd ≈ 0.47. | 100 mm sphere, 15 m/s, half-model symmetry |
| Sphere, Re = 4×10⁶ | Same shape past the "drag crisis": the boundary layer is turbulent before it separates, Cd ≈ 0.18. | same STL read in cm (1 m), 60 m/s, symmetry |
| Ahmed body, 25° slant | The standard simplified car. The 25° slant sits close to where the flow over the rear window stops reattaching, which makes it notoriously hard for steady RANS solvers. | 1044 mm body, 60 m/s, static ground plane, symmetry |

References: Achenbach (1972), *J. Fluid Mech.* 54, for the sphere; Ahmed, Ramm & Faltin
(1984), SAE 840300, for the Ahmed body (Cd = 0.285 at 25°).

## What the numbers say

**Sphere at Re = 1×10⁵: about a third too low, as expected for this solver.** Drag
climbs as the mesh gets finer (0.290 → 0.301 → 0.319) but stays well under the measured
0.47, and 94–97% of it is pressure drag, so the error is where the flow separates, not
skin friction. At this Reynolds number the real boundary layer is laminar and separates
early, around 80° from the front stagnation point, leaving a wide wake. k-ω SST treats
the boundary layer as turbulent from the start, which holds the flow on longer, narrows
the wake and cuts pressure drag, pushing the result toward the lower drag of a faster
sphere. The sweep also didn't settle by fine (5.7% change), so a finer mesh would move
this a little further toward the measurement, but not close the gap.

**Sphere at Re = 4×10⁶: about 45% too high.** Past the drag crisis the real boundary
layer is turbulent, so this was the case expected to suit k-ω SST. It didn't: Cd came
out 0.257 on coarse and 0.261 on medium (1.4% apart, so the mesh isn't the problem),
against about 0.18 measured. The coarse run's Cd was still wobbling (±0.015) when its
iterations ran out, so treat the medium value as the result (the sweep's "independent at
coarse" verdict above counts that unconverged run, which is a gap in the sweep logic, not
in the result). The drag is again 96%
pressure drag: here the solver separates the flow too early and leaves too wide a wake,
the opposite error from the slow sphere.

Put the two sphere cases together and the pattern is clear. WindTunnel's sphere drag
barely changes with Reynolds number (0.26 to 0.32), while a real sphere drops from 0.47 to
about 0.18 through the drag crisis. Smooth curved surfaces, where the separation point is
free to move, are the weakest case for this solver. Bodies with sharp edges that fix where
the flow separates, like the Ahmed body's base and slant, come out much closer.

**Ahmed body, 25°: within 9%, and mesh independent.** Drag dropped from coarse to
medium (0.328 → 0.306) and then held within 1.4% on the fine mesh (0.311), so the sweep
stopped at a converged answer on a 0.7M-cell half model. That is 9% above the measured
0.285. About 87% of the drag is pressure drag on every mesh, so again the difference is in
the separated flow rather than skin friction. Overpredicting drag on the 25° slant is the
typical result for steady k-ω SST, because 25° sits right at the angle where the real flow
over the slant switches between reattaching and fully separating, and steady RANS tends to
get that switch wrong. The frontal area WindTunnel measured from the STL, 0.1120 m², matches
the published reference area of 0.112 m², so the geometry and coefficients line up.

## Known limitations, stated plainly

- **Steady RANS with k-ω SST treats every boundary layer as turbulent.** Real flow at low
  Reynolds number starts laminar and separates earlier, with more drag. That is a large
  part of why the Re = 1×10⁵ sphere comes out a third below its measured drag. Nothing in
  the mesh fixes this; it needs a transition model, which WindTunnel doesn't have yet.
  Small, slow models (RC aircraft at low speed, pinewood derby cars) are in this regime,
  so trust comparisons between designs more than absolute drag there.
- **Steady solves of unsteady wakes.** Bluff-body wakes shed vortices. A steady solver
  returns an averaged-looking flow and the Cd settles with some wobble; WindTunnel reports
  Cd averaged over the last 20% of iterations and its spread.
- **The Ahmed body is modeled without its four support stilts**, and the app places the
  floor 12% of the body height (35 mm) below it instead of the experiment's 50 mm.
- **Wall functions, not resolved boundary layers.** Three prism layers keep meshes small
  enough for a laptop, which limits skin-friction accuracy.
